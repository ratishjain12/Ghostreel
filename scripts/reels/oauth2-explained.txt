You've clicked "Continue with Google" a thousand times. Here's what actually happens in those two seconds.

OAuth 2.0 isn't for logging you in — it's for letting one app hand another app limited access, without ever sharing your password. That distinction matters more than people think.

Say a photo printing app wants access to your Google Photos. Without OAuth, you'd have to hand over your actual Google password — which means that app can now do literally anything your account can do. Terrible idea.

Instead, OAuth redirects you to Google itself. You log in there, on Google's page, not the printing app's page. Google asks: this app wants to read your photos, allow it? You say yes, and Google hands the printing app a short-lived access token — not your password, just a scoped permission slip.

That token says exactly one thing: this app can read your photos, for this long, and nothing else. It can't change your password, it can't read your email, it can't do anything outside that scope.

And when the token expires? The app uses a longer-lived refresh token to quietly get a new one, without bothering you again.
